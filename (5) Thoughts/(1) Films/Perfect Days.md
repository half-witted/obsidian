<p align="justify">In today’s society, ambition is often portrayed as the highest virtue, something to constantly chase, prove, and display. Movies, social media, and other forms of media often celebrate characters who are always striving for more: more success, more money, etc. This constant push towards greater can quietly shape how we see our own lives, making stillness and simplicity feel like failure. This results in many people forgetting to appreciate what they already have, always looking ahead to the next achievement instead of acknowledging the value of the present moment. Slow, simple living gets pushed aside and the beauty of ordinary, what people call ‘boring’ life is easily overlooked just to live up to these endless ambitions. People begin to focus only on the light at the end of the tunnel, without noticing the meaning and beauty that exists within the tunnel itself.</p>
<p align="justify">Perfect Days aligns really well with my thought, in fact, it gives me so many meanings to slow and simple living for me to carry on doing it even more. What first appears to be a simple story of a man living slowly and quietly, eventually reveals a deeper philosophy behind that. I have many terms or symbolism I’ve found out about this film, I want to address some of them.</p>
<p align="justify">One is called “Mushin”, a deeply rooted concept coming from Zen Buddhism. The first character “Mu” represents negation as in not or without, the second character “Shin” represents the heart-mind. It sounds crazy at first for having no heart or mind, cause what do you mean having no mind? Having to live without thinking about others? But this thing is different from heartless or mindless, this one teaches us to be free from thought and attachment. It means being so fully present in what you’re doing, free from worry about the past or anxiety about the future. This allows us to find and appreciate little moments of peace amongst what each day brings.
</p>
<p align="justify">In a world full of noise and simultaneity, Hirayama chooses a slow and simple life. He embodies this philosophy as closely as one can while still participating in society. Despite the ups and downs of his life, he wakes up each day with the same appreciation and curiosity. Scenes where the moments of his day shift from colour into monochrome as he hits the sack. Colour, as I interpret it, is being fully alive inside an experience. When the day ends, it becomes a memory (still present, not erased, not forgotten) but no longer vivid because he’s no longer inside of it. The colour fades because he’s already moved into the upcoming moments while still keeping the past ones. “Next time is next time, now is now” captures this beautifully, a quiet reminder to be fully present rather than saving yourself for some future moment. This is quietly, “Mushin”, he’s completely present while a moment is happening, and when it passes, he carries it lightly without clinging to it.
</p>
<p align="justify">There's a shot near the very end of the film where Hirayama is driving and crying and something close to smiling all at once. Not one after the other, but at the same time, on the same face. After researching the fancy word for it (like I always do), i found the word “Komorebi”, a Japanese word for the shimmering light and shadows that is created by leaves swaying in the wind. Every kindness, every greatness, every good thing, comes with its own contradicton. Chase happiness long enough and you bump into depression. Each attempt at enlightenment casts a long shadow.</p>
<p align="justify">As we deep dive into the film, we realise that Hirayama isn’t perfect and the life he’s chosen isn’t without its falls. He’s not a benevolent, undefeated God, he’s a human being. And to be human, is to be flawed, to cry, to laugh. But what makes him different is how he takes it and embraces it as a part of his life. Hirayama understands this perhaps better than anyone. His daily routine isn’t an escape from shadow, but a quiet acceptance of it. That final shot is the proof of it. Notice how he’s crying and laughing at the same time, indicating that his joy and sorrow aren’t opposites, but two things to exist the same way light and shadow exist in the same leaf swaying in the wind.</p>
<p align="justify">We live in a society that’s trapped in the idea of a perfect, shadow-free life. One where suffering is a problem to be solved, sadness is a disorder to be cured, and stillness is a gap to be filled. This world gives a bad impression to shadow as if it’s an enemy to be defeated. Fantasy movies often describe light as a hero and shadow as a villain, horror movies tell us that bad things come in shadow that it should be avoided. But Komorebi tells us otherwise. The shadow isn’t the enemy of the light, it’s never been. It’s what gives light its shape, depth, and prolly meaning. Without the leaves casting their shade, the light would only blind everyone in its way.</p>
<p align="justify">Perfect Days doesn’t suggest the idea of rejecting ambition or remaining unchanged and having no progress, not a bit. It presents a more balanced way of living where ambition and appreciation being one inseparable unit that mutually influences one another. Hirayama doesn’t reject the ambition, he lets it combined with thankfulness perfectly. In return, he continues to work, fully maintain responsibility, and engage with the world while also appreciating the quiet beauty of his daily routine. His life suggests that true fulfillment lies in balancing the desire to grow with the ability to remain grateful for what already exists.</p>
<p align="justify">This idea closely aligns with the concept in Islam that encourages thriving in life while maintaining gratitude. In surah Al Qasas (28:77), Allah says, “But seek, through that which Allah has given you, the home of the Hereafter; and do not forget your share of the world.” This verse doesn’t order Muslims to abandon ambition. Rather, it reminds us to use of the blessings and opportunities Allah has given us while ensuring that our ambitions never distract us from our true purpose. Success shouldn’t make our prayers be the very price to pay, our worship, our remembrance of Allah, for ambition without gratitude can easily turn into greed for this temporary world. Instead, Islam teaches that every achievement should come with thankfulness, resulting in us being closer to our Creator. Islam shows that both ambition and appreciation can exist together without contradiction.
</p>
<p align="justify">This is the reason why Perfect Days stays in the deepest part of my heart, and why I make it my favourite film in Letterboxd. It reminds me that a slow, what many people would call ‘boring’ life isn’t an empty one. I’ve been living a quite slow life for some time now, and I find meaning in activities that people often overlook or hate, such as reading books, writing journals (including this one), or simply just observing the quiet beauty of nature.
</p>
<p align="justify">It also changed the way I see myself. I used to be a perfectionist, where everything I did had to be perfect no matter what the circumstances were. I would spend too much time and energy trying to achieve an impossible standard, often ignoring the enjoyment of the things I was doing.</p>
