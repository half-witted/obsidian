<p align="justify">When you think about romance films, there's always a problem at the center of them. In Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, Joel and Clementine love each other but also keep destroying each other. In Falling in Love Like in Movies, Bagus has to deal with the difficulty of Hana's grief. The conflict is what drives the story forward and without it, most romance films would've nothing to say. But the Before Trilogy says otherwise. </p>

<p align="justify">There's no bad guy her, no betrayal, no misconceptions, no dramatic sequences, nothing. Just two people and conversations. Romance doesn't need a crisis for it to be interesting, sometimes the most honest a love story can do is simply exist, the way rel-life relationships do. You meet someone, you talk, you feel something, you click, that's it.</p>

<p align="justify">People call this trilogy a yapping trilogy, but the yapping is the whole point of it. Jesse and Celine don't resolve their problems through dramatic gestures. When something unsettling for them rises up, they talk about it. When they get angry at one another, they talk about it. That's exactly what people in actual relationships do. Most films make conversation as a part of the dramatic moment, but here, the conversation is the dramatic momen</p>

<p align="justify">Before Sunrise is the purest and most romantic of the three. It captures how easy it can be to fall in love with someone. No dramatic moment, dramatic gesture that makes Jesse and Celine click, they basically just click. It reminds you that sometimes all it takes is the right person and the right night, and everything else follows naturally. What's even more interesting is how they handle differences. They may have different opinions about things, Celine has a different vision of feminism than Jesse. But what do they do? Yes, they talk about it. They don't try to convince one another to agree with each other's opinion. Instead, they try to find a point where they both agree without abandoning their visions, win-win solution people may call.</p>

<p align="justify">Before Sunset is the best of the three in my opinion. Jesse and Celine meet again after nine years and spend the whole film wondering around the one thing they can't stop thinking: what could've been if they'd stayed together. Every conversation carries that weight underneath it. They're both older, both clearly still affected by each other in ways they're trying not to show. But what gets me is the small details. Even after nine years, they still remember the very little details about each other. it's a reminder that the purest love isn't always found in people who never left one another, sometimes distance just proves how real a relationship is.
</p>
<p align="justify">Before midnight is the most honest of the three. The butterflies are gone and what's left is something messier and more real. Jesse and Celine argue, say things they don't mean to, irritate each other, and then find their way back. That's exactly the point. A lot of people see problems in a relationship as a sign that it's over, that love has run out. But this film quietly breaks this idea. The argument isn't the end of Jesse and Celine's love journey, it's proof that they're still in it, still invested enough to fight, still choosing each other. Problems don't mean a relationship is broken, sometimes they just mean it's real.</p>

<p align="justify">With all of that being said, the trilogy isn't without its flaws. The biggest issue is that Jesse and Celine can feel almost too articulate, too good at speaking. Every conversation is perfectly formed, they have every response for every statement. It's just too click and too flowing, real people don't talk like this, they say the wrong thing, they don't know what to say. And then there's Celine in Before Midnight. The argument in the hotel room is the most ambiguous scene, but at some point she becomes too hard to follow. Her frustration is valid, but the way she expresses them feels messy, irrational in a way that doesn't match who she was in the first two films.</p>
