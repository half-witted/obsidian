<p align="justify">After watching Hamnet, I kinda changed my perspective on Chloe Zhao. Before this, I thought she was a terrible director. You’ll know what I mean after watching Eternals. For me, it’s easily one of the worst Marvel movies ever made. Most marvel movies already feel horrendous, but it was somehow even worse. Everything felt rushed, it tried to introduce too many characters (prolly about ten characters), without giving us enough time to truly know or care about them. Being a series would be much better for this movie as the characters could be explored and known more than in a movie. The only one thing I genuinely liked was Makkari’s speedster scenes, unlike most speedster scenes that rely on endless slow motion, her powers felt fast and powerful.
</p>
Hamnet actually surprised me.
<p align="justify">What I love about this movie is its vast collection of meanings and symbolism which isn’t possible for me to address them one by one, but I have some of my favourites. The first one is how people deal with grief differently. One person may express their grief by crying and breaking out loud just like how Agnes does, one may express it by staying silent, burying their emotions deep inside themselves. Neither way is more correct than the other, the film simply shows that grief is deeply personal that everyone carries it in their own way.
</p>
<p align="justify">It teaches us how stigmas and expectations can slowly change people entirely. Agnes believes that grieving should be visible, that people should cry until their last teardrop. Because of that belief, she finds it difficult to understand William’s silence and emotional distance in dealing with grief, seeing it almost as a sign that he doesn’t give a fuck enough. But in reality, William is grieving too, just in a quieter way. Just like how stigmas work in real life, men are told not to cry in every situation, even in grief. This makes men unable to express their emotions fully to others, they tend to keep them hidden inside their hearts, without knowing that this action may cause bizarre consequences.
</p>
<p align="justify">The second theme I really love is how the movie suggests that holding on to the death too tightly can sometimes make the pain even worse, both for the living and the dead. In Hamnet, death is often symbolised by a dark cave, a place where souls peacefully enter the afterlife. Instead of portraying death as something violent or horrifying, this film reminds us that death should eventually be accepted.</p>
<p align="justify">The idea becomes stronger through the story of Orpheus and Eurydice which is referenced throughout the film. Orpheus, unable to accept Eurydice’s death, goes into the underworld and begs Hades to let his beloved one return to the world of the living. Hades complies with his demand but under one condition: Orpheus mustn’t look back at her during their journey back. As they begin their ascent, everything feels silent and hollow. He can’t contain it any longer, turning back, only to end up losing her forever, trapped in the underworld. To me, this suggests that you can’t simply take the dead back to the world of the living by holding tightly onto them.</p>
<p align="justify">The symbolism becomes even more heartbreaking in Hamnet’s death scene. As Hamnet is dying, he reaches out his hand toward Agnes, almost as if he wants his mother to accompany him for the last time into the afterlife. But Agnes is too focused on trying to stop him from dying, opposing destiny itself, rather than taking his hand. This makes Hamnet unable to peacefully move on to the afterlife. Instead, he becomes trapped somewhere between the world of the living and the afterlife, wandering alone and searching for his mother but never truly finding her, Agnes’ inability to let him go traps him there.
</p>
<p align="justify">The movie also subtly suggests that true acceptance allows the living and the dead to see one another clearly in a peculiar way. Earlier in the film, when Agnes’ falcon dies, she sincerely accepts its death and is later able to see it clearly. But when Hamnet dies, Agnes can’t truly accept his death, making them unable to see each other. Even though Hamnet searches back and forth for her while trapped between worlds, they can’t reach each other as long as Agnes still refuses to truly accept his death.</p>
<p align="justify">Only later, during the performance of The Tragedy of Hamlet created by William, Agnes finally understands William’s grief as well as her own incompetence to let Hamnet go. In the final act of the play, she holds the actor who plays Hamlet, indicating that she finally accepts Hamnet’s death and is ready to accompany him into the afterlife. Eventually, they are finally able to see each other, and Hamnet can peacefully enter the cave and move on to the afterlife.
</p>
<p align="justify">This film is also aligned with how Islam deals with death and grief. Muhammad once told his companions not to cry over the dead, they accepted his words faithfully. However, when someone close to him passed away, he himself cried. This made his companions questioned his actions after telling them not to do so in prior. He then explained to them that crying itself isn’t forbidden, because sadness and tears are part of human nature. What’s not allowed is crying in a way that opposes Allah’s destiny, questioning why someone had to die, why death came too soon, and why fate chose that person instead of someone else. Grieving because we miss someone, feel lonely without them, or feel the emptiness they leave behind is completely human. But turning that grief into anger against destiny is what becomes dangerous.</p>
